    Notes on block space

    A block is a page with a hard word limit. Every four hundred milliseconds the network binds one, and everything in the world that wants to be remembered competes for the same few million bytes. Instruction data, a swap, a wedding message, a Mad Lad — all of it bids against everything else for room that never gets bigger on purpose.

    Writing for a page like that changes how you write. Not because a smarter style emerges, but because a price does.

    Pay for what you mean

    Most prose is instruction data. It exists to move the conversation one step forward and could be discarded after. A little of it is state: it should still be readable in thirty years, by someone with no context and no patience. The mistake is paying long-term prices for short-term bytes — publishing permanent, immutable, timestamped versions of things you would not say twice.

    The chain does not reward the loudest writer. It rewards the one who keeps saying the same thing and keeps being right.

    Compression is a moral habit

    Watch a good program engineer and you will see a kind of courtesy: short function names, dense events, nothing decorative. Not to save lamports — well, to save lamports — but the habit spills over. When every byte has a price, you learn which of your sentences were freight. It is remarkable how much of a first draft was freight.

    This essay, for instance, first came in at three times the size. It had a section about merkle trees. Nobody bid for it.

    The page turns anyway

    The strange comfort of block space is that it is forgiven by time. Each block buries the last a little deeper. The leader's queue — the slush of everything everyone wants to say right now — is the only part that feels urgent, and it is cleared every four hundred milliseconds forever.

    So: write like the page has a limit, because it does. Mean what you mint. And when a paragraph turns out to be dust, let it stay unspent.
