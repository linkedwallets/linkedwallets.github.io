    Here is a detail of the machinery most people never meet. When a wallet spends, it does not shave a little off the top, the way a bank account does. It burns the whole note and sends you the change back — to a brand-new address that did not exist a second ago. Same keys behind it, same owner, fresh face. The ledger calls it a change address. It is the system quietly handing you a new identity every time you buy something.

    I have been thinking about that mechanism as a way to live.

    The address is not the wallet

    We make the same category error about ourselves that newcomers make about chains: confusing the address with the wallet. An address is one pocket, one moment, one pattern of spending. The wallet is the seed beneath it, capable of deriving a thousand more. Lose the distinction and every change of address feels like death. Keep it and a move is just a derivation — the same you, expressed at a new coordinate.

    You are not your history on one address. You are the key that can always derive the next one.

    Forwarding, not forgetting

    A change address is not an amnesty. The old address keeps its history forever, public and perfectly legible. Anyone curious can still see what was spent and where the change went. That is the honest version of starting over: the past stays on the record, but the spending pattern — the shape of your days — begins again somewhere unread.

    People who reinvent themselves badly try to burn the old address and pretend the transaction never happened. The chain does not allow it, and neither, really, do people. What both allow is carrying the change forward: keeping what was left over, spending it differently.

    Spend something

    There is one more clause in the mechanism, and it is the strictest: a change address only comes into existence when you actually spend. No transaction, no new pocket. The system withholds the fresh start from anyone who holds everything and moves nothing.

    So when I feel stuck, I try to remember how a wallet unsticks itself. It picks something worth sending. It pays the gas, burns the note, and lets the remainder land somewhere it has never been. Then it checks the new balance — always a little lighter than before — and starts spending from there.
